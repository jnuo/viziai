**TSH** — thyroid-stimulating hormone — is the signal your pituitary gland sends to your thyroid. When TSH is high, your pituitary is shouting at your thyroid to produce more hormones. When TSH is low, it has backed off because the thyroid is already overproducing. That single number on your blood test report is a direct window into your thyroid's workload. Thyroid disorders affect an estimated **200 million people worldwide**, and most diagnoses start with a TSH measurement. Whether you've just seen a flagged result for the first time or you've been tracking thyroid function for years, this guide explains what the numbers mean, what drives them out of range, and how to use trends rather than single snapshots to understand your thyroid health.

## What Is TSH?

**Thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH)** is produced by the **pituitary gland**, a small structure at the base of the brain. Its job is to regulate the thyroid gland by telling it how much T3 (triiodothyronine) and T4 (thyroxine) to produce. These thyroid hormones control your metabolic rate, heart rate, body temperature, and energy production throughout every cell in your body.

The system works like a thermostat: when T3 and T4 levels fall too low, the pituitary ramps up TSH to push the thyroid harder. When thyroid hormones reach a sufficient level, the pituitary backs off and TSH drops. This feedback loop — the **hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid axis** — recalibrates continuously.

The American Thyroid Association (ATA) designates TSH as the **first-line test** for evaluating thyroid function. It is more sensitive than measuring T3 or T4 directly, because TSH changes appear earlier — often before free T4 has moved outside the reference range. The ATA recommends TSH screening starting at age **35**, repeated every **5 years** in people without known thyroid disease.

## Normal TSH Levels by Age and Group

Reference ranges differ slightly between laboratories and clinical guidelines. These are the widely used values:

| Group | Normal TSH Range |
|-------|-----------------|
| Adults (18–60 years) | **0.4–4.0 mIU/L** |
| Adults over 60 | **0.4–6.0 mIU/L** (some guidelines) |
| Pregnancy – 1st trimester | **0.1–2.5 mIU/L** |
| Pregnancy – 2nd trimester | **0.2–3.0 mIU/L** |
| Pregnancy – 3rd trimester | **0.3–3.5 mIU/L** |
| Newborns | 1.0–39.0 mIU/L |

Key thresholds to know:

- **> 4.5 mIU/L**: Hypothyroidism range — warrants clinical evaluation
- **4.5–10 mIU/L**: Subclinical hypothyroidism — many people have no symptoms
- **> 10 mIU/L**: Overt hypothyroidism — treatment is generally recommended
- **< 0.4 mIU/L**: Hyperthyroidism range
- **< 0.1 mIU/L**: Overt hyperthyroidism — may require urgent evaluation

A single TSH result tells you where you stand right now. A **trend over time** tells you which direction you're moving. A TSH of 3.8 mIU/L is less concerning when it's been stable for three years than when it was 1.2 mIU/L eighteen months ago.

## High TSH: Causes and Symptoms of Hypothyroidism

When TSH is above the upper limit of normal, the thyroid gland is not producing enough T3 and T4 to meet demand. This condition is called **hypothyroidism**. The pituitary keeps sending stronger and stronger TSH signals, but the thyroid cannot keep up.

Hypothyroidism affects an estimated **5% of the U.S. population**, and is **5 to 8 times more common in women** than men. Subclinical hypothyroidism — elevated TSH with normal free T4, often without symptoms — affects an additional **4–8%** of adults.

### Common Causes of High TSH

**Hashimoto's thyroiditis** is the most frequent cause in developed countries. In this autoimmune condition, the immune system attacks thyroid tissue, gradually reducing its ability to produce hormones. **Anti-TPO antibodies** and anti-thyroglobulin antibodies are elevated in most cases. Hashimoto's often develops slowly over years before TSH moves out of range.

**Thyroid surgery or radioactive iodine (RAI) treatment** removes or destroys thyroid tissue. What remains may not produce sufficient T3 and T4, leading to chronically elevated TSH. These patients typically require lifelong levothyroxine replacement.

**Medications** can suppress thyroid function and raise TSH. Lithium (used in bipolar disorder), amiodarone (a cardiac medication containing high amounts of iodine), and certain chemotherapy drugs are common culprits.

**Iodine deficiency** remains the leading cause of hypothyroidism globally, particularly in landlocked regions with low seafood consumption. Where iodized salt programs are in place, this cause is much less common.

### Symptoms of Hypothyroidism

**Fatigue and weakness** are the most consistently reported complaints. When thyroid hormone levels fall, every metabolic process slows: cells produce less energy, body temperature drops, and the cardiovascular system works at a reduced pace. A systematic review found that **clinically significant depressive symptoms** were present in **60% of hypothyroid patients** before treatment was started (Bauer et al., 2002, *Journal of Affective Disorders*).

**Cold intolerance** — feeling chilly when others are comfortable — is one of the most specific symptoms. **Unexplained weight gain** without an increase in appetite is another hallmark, because a slower metabolic rate means the body burns fewer calories even at rest.

**Hair loss, dry skin, and brittle nails** reflect the downstream effects of low thyroid hormone on rapidly dividing cells. **Constipation** is common because intestinal motility slows. **Brain fog**, slow thinking, and difficulty concentrating are frequent complaints that often improve substantially once TSH is normalized through treatment.

Severe, untreated hypothyroidism can lead to **myxedema** — facial and eyelid puffiness with a deepened voice — and in rare cases, myxedema coma, a life-threatening emergency.

## Low TSH: Causes and Symptoms of Hyperthyroidism

When TSH falls below the lower limit of normal, the thyroid is producing too much hormone — a condition called **hyperthyroidism**. The pituitary has suppressed TSH because circulating T3 and T4 are already too high.

Hyperthyroidism affects about **1–2% of the general population** and is also more common in women. Unlike hypothyroidism, which often develops over years, hyperthyroidism can produce noticeable symptoms relatively quickly.

### Common Causes of Low TSH

**Graves' disease** accounts for **60–80% of all hyperthyroidism cases** worldwide. This autoimmune disorder produces TSH-receptor antibodies (TRAb) that mimic TSH and continuously stimulate the thyroid to overproduce hormones. The **TRAb test** is used to confirm the diagnosis.

**Toxic nodular goiter** is more common in older adults over 60. Autonomous thyroid nodules produce hormones independently of pituitary control, bypassing the normal feedback loop.

**Excessive thyroid hormone medication** is a preventable cause. If the levothyroxine dose is too high, TSH will be suppressed into the hyperthyroid range. This is why patients on thyroid hormone replacement should have TSH checked every **6–12 months**.

**Postpartum thyroiditis** causes transient hyperthyroidism in roughly **5–7% of women** in the first year after delivery, often followed by a hypothyroid phase before the thyroid normalizes.

### Symptoms of Hyperthyroidism

**Heart palpitations and rapid heart rate** (tachycardia) are among the most prominent signs. Resting heart rate may sit above **90–110 beats per minute**, and some patients develop atrial fibrillation — hyperthyroidism increases AF risk by **2–3 times** compared to the general population (Frost et al., 2004, *Annals of Internal Medicine*).

**Anxiety, irritability, and insomnia** are consistent features. The constant stimulation of the sympathetic nervous system produces a state of persistent restlessness. **Hand tremors** are easily detected by holding a piece of paper on an outstretched hand.

**Unintentional weight loss** despite an increased appetite is characteristic — metabolic rate accelerates so dramatically that caloric intake can't keep up. **Excessive sweating**, heat intolerance, and **frequent bowel movements** round out the typical picture.

In Graves' disease specifically, **proptosis** (eyes appearing to bulge forward) can develop due to inflammation and swelling of the eye muscles — this finding is largely unique to Graves' and doesn't occur with other causes of hyperthyroidism.

## When Should You Get a TSH Test?

Testing is recommended in these situations:

- Unexplained fatigue, weight change, mood shifts, or hair loss
- Family history of thyroid disease (Hashimoto's or Graves' disease have strong genetic components)
- **Age 35+**: routine baseline TSH, then every 5 years if normal (ATA)
- Before and during **pregnancy** — untreated hypothyroidism raises the risk of miscarriage, preterm birth, and impaired fetal brain development
- If you take lithium, amiodarone, or other thyroid-affecting medications
- Monitoring **if you're on thyroid hormone replacement** — typically every 6–12 months after dose stabilization
- Presence of another autoimmune condition (Type 1 diabetes, rheumatoid arthritis, celiac disease)

Thyroid symptoms are often nonspecific — fatigue and mood changes point to a long list of conditions. A TSH test is quick, inexpensive, and frequently the fastest path to a diagnosis.

## Tracking Your TSH Over Time

A TSH result without context is a snapshot. A sequence of results is a story. If your TSH was 1.8 mIU/L two years ago and it's now 4.2 mIU/L, that upward trend matters — even if 4.2 mIU/L is still technically within the reference range of some labs. If you're on levothyroxine and your TSH has been stable at 1.5 mIU/L for 18 months, you have evidence that your current dose is working.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What TSH level is considered too high?**

The most widely used cutoff is **4.0–4.5 mIU/L**, though this varies by lab. Between 4.5 and 10 mIU/L is called **subclinical hypothyroidism** — many people have no noticeable symptoms in this range, and the decision to treat depends on individual factors including symptoms, age, cardiovascular risk, and whether anti-TPO antibodies are positive. Above **10 mIU/L** is overt hypothyroidism, and most guidelines recommend starting levothyroxine treatment at this level. A single high TSH result should generally be confirmed with a repeat test **4–6 weeks later**, because transient elevations can occur with acute illness, lab error, or certain medications. In pregnancy, the thresholds are tighter: TSH should stay **below 2.5 mIU/L** in the first trimester.

**What does a low TSH level mean?**

A TSH **below 0.4 mIU/L** indicates the thyroid is producing more hormone than the body needs — a state called **hyperthyroidism**. Graves' disease causes the majority of cases. A borderline-low TSH (0.1–0.4 mIU/L) is called subclinical hyperthyroidism; it may be asymptomatic but still carries an increased risk of atrial fibrillation (approximately **3-fold higher** risk) and reduced bone density with long-term exposure. If you're on levothyroxine and your TSH is low, it likely means your dose needs reduction. Your doctor will order free T4 and usually T3 alongside the TSH to determine whether treatment is needed and which kind.

**Do I need to fast before a TSH test?**

Fasting is not required for a TSH test, but **morning testing is preferable**. TSH levels follow a circadian rhythm — they peak between 2–4 AM and are at their lowest in the afternoon and evening. This variation can be as large as **25–30%**, which matters when results fall close to a decision threshold. If you take biotin (vitamin B7) supplements, stop them **48–72 hours before** your test — high-dose biotin can interfere with the immunoassay and produce falsely low TSH readings. If you're on thyroid hormone replacement, take your medication as usual on the morning of the test and have blood drawn at least **4–6 hours later**, or follow your doctor's specific instructions.

**How does TSH change during pregnancy?**

During pregnancy, TSH reference ranges shift substantially. **Human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG)**, which rises sharply in the first trimester, has a weak TSH-like effect on the thyroid — this can transiently lower TSH in early pregnancy even in healthy women. The ATA recommends that TSH stay below **2.5 mIU/L in the first trimester** and below **3.0 mIU/L** in the second and third trimesters. Untreated maternal hypothyroidism is associated with **a 2–3 times higher risk of miscarriage** and measurable deficits in child IQ (Haddow et al., 1999, *NEJM*). If you were already on levothyroxine before pregnancy, your dose will likely need to increase by **25–30%** — often as soon as pregnancy is confirmed. TSH should be checked every **4 weeks** through mid-pregnancy and at least once in the third trimester.

**What's the difference between TSH, free T4, and T3?**

TSH measures what your **pituitary** is signaling — it's a control dial, not the hormone itself. **Free T4 (fT4)** measures the active form of the main thyroid hormone that the thyroid secretes. Together, high TSH + low fT4 confirms overt hypothyroidism; high TSH + normal fT4 suggests subclinical hypothyroidism. **Free T3 (fT3)** reflects the most biologically active thyroid hormone — T4 is converted to T3 in peripheral tissues. T3 is not routinely measured in screening, but it becomes relevant when hyperthyroidism is suspected and T4 is still normal (T3 toxicosis), or when evaluating whether T4-to-T3 conversion is adequate in treated patients. In thyroid cancer follow-up, TSH is often intentionally kept **suppressed below 0.1 mIU/L** with high-dose levothyroxine — in that context, a low TSH is the treatment goal, not a sign of hyperthyroidism.
